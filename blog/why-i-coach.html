Why I Coach: From the South Side to the Weight Room

By D'Vante Glenn, CSCS · September 2026 · 6 min read

People ask me how I got into coaching, and they usually expect a story about a big moment — a championship, an injury, a mentor's speech. The truth is quieter than that. I got into coaching because of the hundreds of small moments where somebody decided I was worth their time.

I grew up in Chicago. If you grew up here too, you know what the city gives you: toughness you didn't ask for, loyalty you didn't have to earn, and — if you're lucky — an adult somewhere who sees something in you before you see it in yourself. For me, those adults were coaches. Baseball coaches, mostly. Men who showed up on cold April mornings, who taught me that how you do the small things is how you do everything, who cared whether I got my schoolwork done as much as whether I hit the cutoff man.

Baseball carried me out of the city to Coe College. I got to live the dream every kid on a Chicago diamond has: playing college ball, winning a conference championship, winning a regional championship my senior year, piling into buses with brothers I still talk to every week. But something else happened at Coe that mattered more. I studied kinesiology — the science of human movement — and for the first time, the things my best coaches had taught me by instinct started making sense on paper. Why the drills worked. Why the rest mattered. Why the guys who moved well stayed healthy and the guys who didn't broke down in June.

Somewhere between the film room and the anatomy lab, I realized I didn't love baseball as much as I loved what baseball does to people.

It builds them. Slowly, honestly, without shortcuts. And I realized the thing I wanted to do with my life was stand on the building side of that process.

Learning to coach by coaching

When my playing days ended, Coe gave me my first shot on the other side of the whiteboard — a year as an assistant coach at my alma mater, learning how different the game looks when the at-bats belong to someone else. Then I came home. I joined the baseball staff at Olive-Harvey College on the South Side, and those seasons taught me more about coaching than anything before or since.

We took a program that had won five games and helped build it into one that won twenty-eight. I got to mentor five players who earned all-region honors. But the number I'm proudest of isn't on any stat sheet: it's the guys who learned to show up early, train with intention, and carry themselves like professionals — guys who found out that the standard they held in the weight room could become the standard they held everywhere.

That's when it locked in for me. Wins are rented. Standards are owned. My job as a coach isn't really to produce stronger squats or faster sixty times — those are side effects. My job is to teach people what their own best effort feels like, so they can recognize it and repeat it for the rest of their lives.

Every body deserves a coach

These days my work spans two worlds that look nothing alike and are exactly the same. In the mornings I'm in the weight room at Roosevelt University, coaching NCAA Division II athletes — ballplayers, sprinters, hoopers, linemen. In the afternoons and evenings I'm training private clients in Chicago — professionals, parents, people in their fifties and sixties who decided this is the year they take their health back.

Here's what I've learned: the 20-year-old shortstop and the 58-year-old executive need the same things from me. They need someone who watches every rep like it matters, because it does. They need the truth, delivered with respect. They need a plan that fits their actual life. And they need someone in their corner who genuinely cares how this story goes — not just this month, but in ten years, in thirty.

I hold a championship standard for every client, because every client is training for something that matters more than a trophy: the rest of their life.

So that's why I coach. Because people invested in me on cold mornings in Chicago, and the debt doesn't get paid back — it gets paid forward. Because I've seen what happens to a person when they find out they're capable of more than they thought. And because there's no better seat in the world than the one next to someone the day they surprise themselves.

If you're reading this and wondering whether you're "the kind of person" who works with a coach — you are. Everybody is. Come find out.
