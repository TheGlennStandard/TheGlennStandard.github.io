Baseball

What Baseball Taught Me About Training for Life

By D'Vante Glenn, CSCS · September 2026 · 5 min read

Baseball is a strange game to love. It's slow when you want it fast. It humiliates you in public, on a schedule. The best hitters who ever lived failed two out of every three tries. And yet I'd argue there is no better education — for training, for coaching, for life — than a childhood spent on a diamond.

I played this game from the sandlots of Chicago to a conference championship at Coe College. Then I coached it, at my alma mater and back home at Olive-Harvey, where our staff helped turn a five-win team into a twenty-eight-win team. Somewhere in all those innings, baseball taught me everything I now teach my clients. Here's the short version.

1. You can't cram

You cannot become a hitter the week before the season. The swing you bring to opening day is the swing you built in November, in a cold cage, when nobody was grading you. Fitness works exactly the same way. There is no two-week transformation, no secret program, no supplement that substitutes for months of honest work. The people who look strong in August are the people who trained in February.

When a new client asks me how fast we can get results, I give them the ballplayer's answer: faster than you fear, slower than you hope, and permanent if we do it right.

2. Failure is data, not verdict

Baseball forces you to build a professional relationship with failure. You strike out, and there are eight guys and your mother watching, and the game asks you to walk back to the dugout, make one small adjustment, and believe completely in your next at-bat. That skill — fail, adjust, recommit, without drama — is the single most transferable thing sport teaches.

A missed lift isn't a verdict on who you are. It's information about what to do next. Hitters know this. I coach everyone to know it.

I watch adults quit fitness programs over one bad week — a missed Monday becomes a lost month becomes "I'm just not a gym person." Ballplayers don't get that luxury; there's a game tomorrow. So we build the same rhythm into your training: bad session, short memory, next pitch.

3. Preparation is a form of respect

The coaches who shaped me treated preparation as a character issue. You take pregame grounders like it's the ninth inning. You know the situation before the ball is hit. Showing up unprepared wasn't just bad strategy — it was disrespectful to your teammates, your opponent, and the game itself.

I bring that into every session I coach. My clients get a plan that was written for them, not pulled off a shelf. My athletes get a warm-up that has a purpose they can explain. And I hold myself to the standard first: if I expect your best hour, you're getting mine.

4. The team makes the player

Nobody develops alone. Every good ballplayer is a group project — parents driving to tournaments, coaches throwing BP after hours, teammates pushing you in the offseason. One of the quiet tragedies of adult fitness is how solitary it gets: one person, earbuds in, grinding alone until they stop. It's why most people stop.

Training with a coach isn't a luxury; it's how development has always actually worked. When you train with me, you get what my best coaches gave me — someone who knows your swing, notices when it's off, and cares whether you show up Thursday.

5. Play the long season

Baseball is a long season. Fifty, sixty, a hundred games. You learn quickly that no single day means much, and every day means something. You can't win the season in April, but you can absolutely lose it there — by getting hurt doing something careless, by burning out chasing early numbers.

Your health is the longest season there is. Decades, not months. The winning approach looks the same as it does on the diamond: show up daily, protect your body, stack small wins, and be very suspicious of anything that promises you the pennant by Friday.

I still love this game more than I can say. But what I love most is what it builds in people — and I get to spend my career passing that on, one athlete, one client, one rep at a time.

See you at the rack. — D'Vante
