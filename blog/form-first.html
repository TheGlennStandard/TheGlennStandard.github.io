Form First: The Case for Slowing Down

By D'Vante Glenn, CSCS · September 2026 · 5 min read

Last week I stopped a client three reps into a set of eight. The weight wasn't heavy. He wasn't in danger. He looked at me like I'd taken away his dessert. "That was fine," he said.

"Fine" is exactly the problem.

His third rep was ninety percent of his first rep. His hips had started drifting, his brace had gone soft, and his body — smart, efficient, and completely uninterested in his long-term goals — had found a way to move the bar with less work from the muscles we were actually there to train. If I'd let him grind out five more, he wouldn't have gotten five more reps of training. He'd have gotten five reps of rehearsing a mistake.

Every rep is a vote for the movement pattern you'll own forever. You don't get to choose which reps count. They all count — especially the sloppy ones.

What "form first" actually means

People hear "form first" and picture a coach who's fussy about aesthetics — someone who wants your squat to look like a textbook photo. That's not it. Form isn't about looking right. Form is about three things that matter enormously:

1. Form is where the results live. The muscle you're trying to build only grows if it's the muscle doing the work. When your form breaks down, the load doesn't disappear — it moves. Usually to your lower back, your shoulders, or your knees, which didn't sign up for the job. Clean technique isn't the polite version of training. It's the effective version.

2. Form is your injury insurance. Almost nobody gets hurt on the bar. They get hurt on the pattern — the same small compensation, repeated ten thousand times, until one Tuesday it isn't small anymore. I've watched it end seasons for athletes and derail years for adults. The most valuable thing I can teach any client is a movement library their body can trust under load, under fatigue, and under the chaos of real life.

3. Form is a habit of mind. This is the one nobody talks about. Training with precision teaches you to pay attention — to notice the difference between "done" and "done right." My athletes at Roosevelt hear this constantly: the way you do the empty-bar warm-up is the way you'll do the game-winning play. Champions aren't people who rise to the occasion. They're people whose standard doesn't drop when nobody's watching.

Slower is faster

Here's the part that surprises people: training this way doesn't slow your progress. It compounds it. The client who spends four weeks earning a beautiful hinge pattern doesn't lose four weeks — they unlock every deadlift, swing, and jump for the next forty years. The ballplayer who masters landing mechanics before we chase sprint times doesn't fall behind — he's the one still on the field in August while the shortcut kids are in the training room.

Baseball taught me this before any textbook did. Nobody walks onto a diamond and hits. You take ten thousand swings in the cage, and the coaches who shaped me cared about swing number 9,999 exactly as much as swing number one. The game rewards deep, boring, patient practice — and so does the human body.

What this means if you train with me

It means your first sessions will feel lighter than your ego wants. It means I will stop sets early, and I'll always tell you why. It means we'll celebrate the day your squat gets deeper as loudly as the day it gets heavier. And it means that when we do load the bar — and we will, more than you expect — you'll be moving weight on a foundation that can carry it.

"Fine" reps build fine bodies. We're not here for fine.

Form first. Built to last. — Coach D
