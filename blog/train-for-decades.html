Longevity

Training for the Decades, Not the Summer

By D'Vante Glenn, CSCS · September 2026 · 6 min read

There's a picture the fitness industry has been selling for fifty years: the beach body, the eight-week shred, the "summer arms." I understand why it sells. It's visible, it's fast, and it flatters the part of us that wants results by Friday.

But I want to tell you about a different picture — the one that actually keeps me in this profession. It's a person in their seventies getting up off the floor without using their hands. Carrying their own groceries up the stairs. Catching themselves when they trip on a curb instead of falling. Playing catch with a grandkid, then doing it again the next day because nothing hurts.

That picture is built in the weight room, and it is built decades in advance. This is the most important thing I know as a coach, and it's the reason I care so deeply about every client's habits, form, and consistency — not just their next twelve weeks.

Strength is the retirement account nobody opens

Here's the biology, in plain language. Starting somewhere in our thirties, we all begin losing muscle and strength — slowly at first, then faster with each decade if we do nothing. Bone density follows the same curve. Power — the ability to produce force quickly, which is what saves you when you slip on an icy Chicago sidewalk — declines fastest of all.

None of this is destiny. Every one of those curves bends upward with training, at any age. Strength training is the closest thing medicine has to a longevity drug: it protects bone, preserves muscle, steadies blood sugar, guards your joints, lifts your mood, and — this is the part I wish more people heard — it preserves your independence. The difference between a fall that bruises and a fall that breaks a hip is very often the strength and reaction you built years earlier.

Every rep you do in your thirties and forties is a deposit your seventy-year-old self gets to withdraw. I've never met anyone who regretted saving.

Why "form first" and "decades" are the same idea

If you've read my other essays, you know I coach form obsessively and build habits before intensity. This essay is the reason why. If your training horizon is eight weeks, you can get away with sloppy reps and heroic unsustainable schedules — you'll be done before the bill comes due. If your horizon is fifty years, everything changes:

Joints have to be protected, because you need them for half a century of use. Movement patterns have to be clean, because you're going to repeat them a hundred thousand times. The schedule has to be one you can keep at 35 with kids, at 50 with a career, at 70 with grandkids. And the relationship with training has to be one of care, not punishment — nobody sticks with self-punishment for decades.

This is why I train a 24-year-old outfielder and a 60-year-old executive with the same underlying philosophy. The outfielder is training for this season and for the body he'll live in when baseball is a memory. The executive is training for energy this quarter and for the version of herself who intends to travel at 80. Same principles: move well, get strong, stay consistent, respect recovery.

It is never too early, and it is never too late

Two groups of people, in my experience, most need to hear this essay.

Young athletes and their parents: the training you do now is not just for the next tryout. You are laying down the movement quality, the tissue resilience, and the relationship with training that you'll carry for life. This is why I refuse to shortcut development for a quick number on a radar gun — I've seen what those shortcuts cost at 30.

Adults who think the window closed: it didn't. The research on this is genuinely joyful — people who begin strength training in their fifties, sixties, and beyond see real, meaningful gains in strength, bone, and function. I've watched clients transform their daily lives starting well past the age they'd written themselves off. The best time to start was twenty years ago. The second-best time is this week, and I mean that literally.

The goal is a bigger life

I'll be honest about my ambition, because it's the engine behind everything I do: I want the people I coach to live bigger lives for longer. More years saying yes — to the pickup game, the hike, the flight, the floor where the grandkids are playing. Health isn't the goal; health is the budget, and everything you love gets paid out of it.

So yes — come train, and we'll absolutely get you stronger for this summer. But understand what we're really doing. We're funding your future. Rep by rep, habit by habit, decade by decade.

Train for the body you'll live in. It's the only one you get, and it's counting on you.

— Coach D'Vante Glenn, CSCS · Est. 773
